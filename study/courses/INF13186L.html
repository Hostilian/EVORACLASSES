Aula 2 · guided learning

Understand the interaction before designing it

Work through the ideas below using the small examples. The examples are for practice and are separate from the assessed AlunUÉ project. Source references use the PDF’s page numbers.

One focused block: Read one lesson for 20 minutes, explain it without looking for 10 minutes, then try its practice prompt for 5 minutes. Take a 5-minute break. Choose the next lesson according to what you can explain, rather than treating every lesson as finished.

1. Goals, actions and Norman’s cycle

Aula 2, pages 6–14; the cycle diagram is on page 10.

A domain is the area of work. A goal is the result a person wants. A task describes the actions needed to reach it. “Be awake at 7 am” is a goal; configuring a phone alarm is a task. The user should not need to understand the system’s internal implementation to express that goal.

Norman’s cycle follows the user from a goal to an action and back to an understanding of the result: goal → intention → specify an action sequence → execute → perceive the result → interpret it → evaluate whether the goal was met. The gulf of execution is the gap between what the user intends and the actions the interface allows. The gulf of evaluation is the effort needed to understand the system’s state and decide whether the goal was achieved.

A slip happens when the goal and intended action are appropriate but execution goes wrong, such as touching the wrong adjacent button. A mistake comes from an incorrect goal or understanding, such as assuming a bell icon means “send a message”. Clear controls and feedback address different points in the cycle.

Try it: You want a phone alarm for 7 am tomorrow. Describe all seven stages. If you tap Save but see no confirmation, which gulf is the main difficulty? If the right control is hard to find, which gulf is involved?

Check the practice reasoning

A possible sequence is: want to wake at 7; intend to set an alarm; plan to select 7 am and save; tap those controls; see an active alarm; understand that it is scheduled for tomorrow; judge that it meets the goal. Missing confirmation mainly creates an evaluation difficulty. Finding how to express the intended action is an execution difficulty. A good explanation identifies the stage and the reason, rather than only naming the gulf.

2. Four translations in Abowd–Beale

Aula 2, pages 15–18; translation arrows are on page 16.

The framework examines communication between four components: User (task language), Input, System (core language) and Output. Each component has its own way of representing information. Interaction problems can arise while translating from one representation to another.

  1. Articulation — User → Input: the person expresses the desired action through the controls.
  2. Performance — Input → System: the input becomes an instruction that the system can execute.
  3. Presentation — System → Output: the system’s new state is represented in the interface.
  4. Observation — Output → User: the person sees and understands that representation.

Try it: A simple room thermostat has temperature buttons and a display. Identify the four components. Describe a different possible failure for each translation. Keep “what the system does” separate from “what its display communicates”.

Check with an unrelated thermostat example

The person is the user, buttons are input, temperature-control logic is the system and the display is output. Unclear button labels can hinder articulation. A valid button press that the controller cannot support creates a performance problem. Failing to display the newly selected setting is a presentation problem. Confusing the displayed room temperature with the target setting is an observation problem. These are illustrative examples, not claims about a particular product.

3. Ergonomics, dialogue and context

Aula 2, pages 19–27.

Ergonomics concerns the physical conditions of interaction: the arrangement of controls and displays, the surroundings, posture, lighting and use of colour. Pages 21 and 27 also ask us to consider different users, colour blindness, social pressure, privacy and motivation. A visually attractive interface can still be difficult to use in its actual setting.

Look and feel includes behaviour as well as appearance. WIMP interfaces use windows, icons, menus and pointers, but similar-looking systems may behave differently. A modal dialogue temporarily takes the initiative away from the user; it should serve a necessary step and be used carefully. Speech interfaces need robust dialogue, confirmation of the system’s understanding and a way to recover when recognition is wrong.

Try it: A noisy self-service kiosk shows a tiny red warning and announces an error aloud. Name one physical/context problem and propose a way for the user to recognise and recover from the error.

Check the practice reasoning

Noise may hide the spoken message; small text or reliance on red may hide the visual warning. A readable message with a clear label and recovery action gives another way to understand the problem. Explain how the change helps a particular user in a particular setting, rather than simply saying the interface should be prettier.

4. Usability goals and user experience

Aula 2, pages 28–37.

The lecture defines usability in relation to specific users, specific goals and a specific context. Its three central dimensions are effectiveness (how well the goal is achieved), efficiency (the resources consumed in achieving it) and satisfaction (comfort and feelings during use). A fast interaction that produces the wrong result is not effective.

Pages 33–34 identify Nielsen’s learnability, efficiency, memorability, error prevention/recovery and satisfaction. Usability goals tend to describe task performance more specifically; user-experience goals also concern qualities such as enjoyment, motivation and usefulness. They can overlap: satisfaction matters to both.

Goals can conflict. Extra confirmations may prevent mistakes but add time; an interface optimised for experts may be harder for a beginner to learn. State the user group and the consequence of a tradeoff instead of assuming one design is best for everyone.

Try it: Compare setting a first alarm with setting an alarm every day. Which usability goals matter in each case? Explain one possible learnability–efficiency tradeoff.

Check the practice reasoning

A beginner may need understandable labels and guidance; a frequent user may value a shorter action sequence. A guided setup can help learning while adding steps for a person who already knows the task. Success, time, errors and reported satisfaction would provide different evidence about the two experiences.

5. Turn “easy to use” into a measurable criterion

Aula 2, pages 38–39 and 53–57.

Usability engineering specifies desired characteristics early and measures them throughout development. “Easy to use” is too vague on its own. Define the user group, task, context, metric and target, then observe representative users carrying out predetermined tasks. The slides list task time, errors, help consultations, successful tasks and clicks as possible measures. Their numerical examples are examples, not universal pass thresholds.

An illustrative target for a basic timer might be: “At least 9 of 10 first-time users can start a two-minute timer without assistance in a quiet room.” This describes task success. Time and satisfaction would require separate measures. It is a made-up practice target, not a research finding or an AlunUÉ project requirement.

Try it: Rewrite “the timer should be quick and pleasant” as one efficiency criterion and one satisfaction criterion. Identify what you would actually record and when.

Check the structure, not a universal number

For efficiency, specify the users and task, then a time measure such as the median time to start the timer under stated conditions. For satisfaction, specify a question and rating scale administered after the task. Choose targets for the scenario and explain them. Do not report invented measurements as observations, and do not treat satisfaction alone as proof of task success.

6. User-centred design is an iterative process

Aula 2, pages 40–59; the four-stage cycle is on pages 47 and 49–52.

The lecture describes lifecycle activities from requirements through design, coding, integration and maintenance. Verification checks whether the product is built correctly against its specification; validation asks whether it is the right product for the real need. Matching a written requirement does not automatically show that the requirement captures what users need.

Interactive design needs feedback and revision. Follow the cycle analyse needs → conceive alternative solutions → prototype → evaluate → return to the needs or solution. Ask who the users are, what they do and where they do it. Early paper prototypes and alternative designs can reveal problems before implementation. Evaluation can involve experts, predictive models or users; these methods provide different kinds of evidence.

The central features are users, measurable usability criteria and repetition. Human error is normal, so design should consider how people behave under stress. With limited time, prioritise problems by their impact and the effort needed to fix them; the aim is useful improvement supported by evidence.

Try it: You are exploring a simple household timer. Plan one small iteration: state a user need, sketch two possible controls, choose a prototype test and say what result would make you revise the design. Give one verification question and one validation question.

Check your plan

The plan should connect a real user/task/context to alternatives and an observation that can change the next version. A verification question might ask whether the timer counts the selected duration accurately. A validation question might ask whether someone cooking can set and notice the timer in the kitchen. Keep the questions distinct and avoid assuming that passing one answers the other.
